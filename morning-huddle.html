Purpose of the morning huddle

The morning huddle is a 15-minute daily team meeting designed to prepare the office for the day before the first patient is seen.

The huddle is not simply a time for announcements. It is our opportunity to review the schedule as a team, identify potential problems, coordinate patient flow, clarify responsibilities, and prevent avoidable delays.

A successful huddle answers all of these

  • Where are the potential problems today?
  • Where are we short-staffed?
  • Are any appointments scheduled incorrectly?
  • Where will doctor time be concentrated?
  • Which patients need extra attention or explanation?
  • Are there procedures a technician is not trained or comfortable performing?
  • Are there lab priorities that affect today's patients?
  • Are there patients with deadlines or special scheduling needs?
  • What needs to be adjusted before the first patient arrives?
  • Who is responsible for each action item?
The goal is to identify problems before they become problems.

Morning huddle rules

These build on the rules that apply to every meeting. Six of them matter most here.

1

Be on time

Everyone attends the huddle on time. The Office Manager is responsible for confirming the team is present and identifying anyone who is late or absent. Repeated lateness is addressed outside of the huddle.

2

Come prepared

The huddle is not the time to begin reviewing the schedule. Anyone responsible for the schedule reviews their area before the meeting begins, and clinical technicians review their own columns beforehand.

3

Bring a notebook

The Front Desk Team and Clinical Team each bring a notebook. Record who was late or absent, what was discussed, the action items for your department, who owns each one, and anything that must be passed to team members arriving later. These notes become the morning huddle minutes.

4

Update anyone who arrives later

Employees who begin work after the huddle are still responsible for knowing what was discussed. When they arrive they review the minutes, speak with their team leader if anything is unclear, and confirm responsibilities or changes affecting their schedule. Information should never be lost because someone was not scheduled to attend.

5

No phones

Phones are not used during the huddle. Everyone should be listening, participating, and preparing for the day.

6

Breakfast before the huddle

Eat before the huddle, not during it. Fifteen minutes requires everyone's full attention.

Everyone participates

The huddle is not a presentation given by one person while everyone else listens. Each department is responsible for reviewing its portion of the day and speaking up when something needs attention. If you notice a scheduling problem, patient-flow problem, doctor-time conflict, training issue, or any other concern, bring it up at the huddle. Do not assume someone else noticed it.

Preparation, role by role

Preparation happens before the huddle begins. The meeting is short because the work is already done when everyone sits down.

Office Manager

Before the meeting

  • Confirm staffing for the day
  • Know who is out
  • Know who is arriving later
  • Identify unexpected call-outs
  • Make sure everyone is present for the huddle
  • Be aware of major office-wide schedule issues

During the huddle

Office-wide announcements limited to today or the immediate future

  • Staffing changes
  • Office procedures
  • Building issues
  • Meetings and important deadlines
  • Schedule changes
  • Operational announcements

Long discussions move to another meeting.

Treatment Coordinator

Schedule-related information that affects office operations

  • Records that may need a records technician
  • Doctor-time conflicts involving consultations
  • Patients who may require a scan after the consultation
  • Consultations that may create schedule-flow concerns
  • Special scheduling arrangements affecting another department

Leave out dentist information, the complete primary concern, dental clearance details, and any financial or personal information that does not affect workflow.

Lab Team Member

The day's lab priorities

  • Appliances needed for today's appointments
  • Appliances that must be completed urgently
  • Repairs
  • Missing appliances
  • Delayed lab cases
  • Cases that may affect today's schedule
  • Items that need doctor review

Anything that could delay a patient's appointment belongs in the huddle.

Marketing Coordinator

Activities that affect the schedule or the team

  • Office events
  • Photography and video recording
  • Social media filming
  • Community events and visitors
  • Promotions requiring team participation
  • Schedule changes related to marketing

Routine updates that do not affect the day's operations should not consume huddle time.

Financial Coordinator

Only what requires action before a patient is seated

  • Patients the clinical or front desk team must handle differently
  • Example: "Please see me before seating the 2:00 patient"

Routine collections and financial detail are not discussed with the whole team. Private financial information stays private.

Team Lead and Management

Management items that affect the team

  • Interviews occurring that day
  • Training activities
  • Calendar updates
  • New team members and employee shadowing
  • Meetings
  • Changes affecting staffing or workflow

Keep it relevant to that day's operation.

Clinic Coordinator and Schedule Coordinator

Perform a full schedule scan before every huddle. Review the clinical schedule from beginning to end and leave the huddle with a clear plan for how the clinic will operate.

Staffing conflicts

  • More clinical columns than available technicians
  • Technician call-outs
  • Team members arriving late
  • Coverage problems
  • Procedures requiring specific trained team members

Appointment problems

Look for appointments with the wrong type, too little or too much time, incorrect procedure scheduling, missing information, or unexpected doctor time.

  • A bonding scheduled when the patient should have an adjustment
  • An adjustment that needs substantially more time than scheduled
  • A high-doctor-time procedure placed next to multiple consultations
  • Several high-doctor-time procedures scheduled at the same time

Doctor-time conflicts

Identify periods when the doctor may be needed in several places at once: bondings, repositions, broken brackets, difficult procedures, procedures requiring significant isolation, consultations, and patients requiring extended conversations. Bring these to the team before patients are seated.

Seating and patient flow

Plan where patients should be seated to create the best flow, considering which technicians are available, procedure difficulty, doctor-time needs, technician experience, patients needing TLC, and which chairs suit particular procedures.

Clinical technicians

Every technician reviews their column before coming to the huddle. Do not wait until the patient is seated to discover a problem.

Incorrectly scheduled appointments

For example, a patient scheduled for a bonding whose treatment notes indicate the next appointment should be an adjustment. Bring it up during the huddle.

Doctor-time conflicts

Look at when your patients require the doctor. If several patients in your column need significant doctor time at once, identify the conflict.

Procedures you are not trained to perform

Tell the team before the patient arrives if your schedule includes a procedure you are not trained or comfortable performing. This lets a trainer or experienced team member be assigned in advance.

  • Removing ceramic brackets
  • Band procedures
  • Aligner attachments
  • Specialized appliance procedures
  • Procedures you have not yet completed independently

Never wait until the procedure has started to announce that assistance is required.

Missing notes or lack of direction

  • Missing previous notes
  • Unclear next-visit instructions
  • Missing treatment directions
  • Treatment cards that do not explain what happens next

Bring these to the huddle so they can be clarified before the patient is seated.

Prepare for your first patient

Before leaving the huddle you should know who your first patient is, what procedure is being performed, what materials are needed, whether the doctor will be needed, and whether any special preparation is necessary. Your chair and tray should be prepared accordingly.

Key topics to identify during the huddle

Six things that break a day. Most of them are visible in the schedule before the day starts.

1Incorrectly scheduled appointments

Some appointments are correctly labeled but need more time than was scheduled. Identify them before the day starts and decide whether additional time can be added, another technician can assist, the patient should be seated differently, or the appointment needs to be modified.

Others have the wrong appointment type entirely: a bonding scheduled instead of an adjustment, an adjustment scheduled when a procedure is actually needed, records scheduled incorrectly, or an appliance procedure under the wrong type. Correct these before the patient arrives whenever possible.

2Doctor-time crunches

Look for periods where the doctor is scheduled to perform several doctor-intensive procedures at once: multiple bondings, repositions, consultations, broken brackets, difficult procedures, or patients requiring extensive explanation.

Ask where the doctor is likely to get backed up today, then build the plan before the schedule reaches that point.

3Difficult or TLC patients

Some patients require additional time, explanation, patience, or assistance. These patients should be scheduled with a Team Leader, an experienced technician, or another designated clinical leader.

If that is not possible, a Team Leader or clinical leader evaluates the situation and helps determine the best plan. The goal is appropriate care for the patient without unnecessary disruption to the rest of the clinic.

4More columns than technicians

This happens through lack of communication about someone being out, last-minute call-outs, scheduling errors, or unexpected staffing changes. Do not wait until patients are arriving to work out how the clinic will handle it.

During the huddle, determine which columns remain open, which technicians will cover them, which patients may need to be moved, whether appointments should be adjusted, whether team leaders need to assist, and where doctor time will be prioritized. Everyone should understand the coverage plan before the first patient.

5Emergencies

Not every emergency requires the same amount of time. Determine what type is being seen and schedule accordingly: a poking wire, loose bracket, broken or lost appliance, significant discomfort, trauma, or another orthodontic concern.

The type of emergency determines appointment length, whether a technician can manage the initial evaluation, whether significant doctor time is required, which chair is appropriate, and whether the schedule needs adjusting.

6Patient deadlines

Identify patients who must leave by a certain time: school and work commitments, transportation schedules, medical appointments, flights and travel, or parent scheduling limitations.

If the team knows in advance, the schedule can usually be managed around it. Do not discover the deadline when the patient is already late leaving.

How the fifteen minutes are spent

Follow the same sequence every day so nothing gets skipped and nobody has to wonder what comes next.

  • Minute 0 to 2

    Staffing and daily overview

    Office Manager and Clinic Coordinator

    • Who is out
    • Who is arriving late
    • Who is arriving later in the day
    • Unexpected call-outs
    • Number of technicians
    • Number of clinical columns
  • Minute 2 to 7

    Clinical schedule

    Clinic Coordinator, Schedule Coordinator, and clinical team. This is the largest portion of the huddle.

    • Incorrectly scheduled appointments
    • Appointments needing additional time
    • Doctor-time conflicts
    • More columns than technicians
    • Seating arrangements
    • Procedures requiring experienced team members
    • Technician training concerns
    • Difficult or TLC patients
    • Emergencies
    • Patient deadlines
    • Missing notes or unclear treatment directions
  • Minute 7 to 9

    Treatment Coordinator

    • Consultation schedule conflicts
    • Records needs
    • Scan needs
    • Doctor-time concerns
    • Anything affecting clinical or front desk workflow
  • Minute 9 to 10

    Lab

    • Today's appliance priorities
    • Missing or delayed cases
    • Repairs
    • Cases affecting today's schedule
  • Minute 10 to 11

    Financial

    Only patients requiring action before they are seated. Routine financial information is not discussed with the whole team.

  • Minute 11 to 12

    Marketing

    • Events
    • Filming and photography
    • Visitors
    • Schedule-related marketing activities
  • Minute 12 to 14

    Office Manager and Team Lead

    • Important announcements
    • Interviews
    • Training
    • Calendar changes
    • Operational information
  • Minute 14 to 15

    Action review

    Before ending, quickly review the most important action items. Every important problem should have an owner and a plan.

    • "Karina is helping Randy with the ceramic debond."
    • "Chair 4 will be used for the 10:30 bonding."
    • "Dr. Lee has a doctor-time conflict at 11:00, so the 11:00 adjustment will be seated first."
    • "Elizabeth needs to speak with the 2:00 patient before they are seated."
    • "The 3:30 patient must leave by 4:15."
    • "Nataly will cover Yeymy's first two patients."

What a successful huddle looks like

Seven qualities. If the huddle is missing one, it is not working yet.

•

Prepared

The schedule has already been reviewed.

•

Brief

The meeting stays close to fifteen minutes.

•

Specific

Discuss actual problems, not vague concerns. Instead of "the afternoon looks busy," say "at 2:00 we have two bondings and a consult requiring doctor time, we need to stagger the seating."

•

Participatory

Everyone speaks up when they identify something important.

•

Solution-oriented

Do not simply identify problems. Determine what we are going to do about it.

•

Documented

Action items and important information are written down.

•

Communicated

Team members arriving later are brought up to speed.

What the morning huddle is not

  • A social meeting
  • Breakfast time
  • Phone time
  • A lengthy management meeting
  • A place for detailed financial discussion
  • A complete review of every patient
  • A time to begin looking at your schedule
  • A substitute for reading patient notes
  • A meeting where one person talks and everyone else stays silent

The team should arrive already familiar with the day's schedule.

The morning huddle standard

Before we see the first patient, the team should know all sixteen of these.

  • Who is working today
  • Who is out
  • Who is arriving later
  • Who is covering each clinical column
  • Where major doctor-time conflicts exist
  • Which appointments are scheduled incorrectly
  • Which appointments may require additional time
  • Which patients require TLC or additional explanation
  • Which technicians require assistance or training
  • Which patients have deadlines
  • Which emergencies have been added
  • What the major lab priorities are
  • Whether any patients need to speak with the financial team before seating
  • Whether consultations affect clinical flow
  • Whether marketing activities affect the schedule
  • What today's major action items are
If we finish the huddle and discover one of these after the patient is already seated, we ask: could we have identified this during the huddle? If the answer is yes, we need to improve our preparation.

Find the problem before the patient finds it for us.

The morning huddle exists so the entire team begins the day with the same information, the same priorities, and a clear plan.

Review. Communicate. Plan. Assign. Execute.
